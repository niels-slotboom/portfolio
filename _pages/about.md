# About Me

Hi! Glad you made it here. I'm Niels Slotboom, a theoretical physicist with a broad range of interests in and around the vast field of gravitation. Ever since I realised how precisely mathematics can describe the physical universe—and how computers can help extract concrete insight through computation and simulation—I've been driven to understand as much of these domains as I can. This website exists to document that journey.

## Academic Background

My academic path began at the University of Bern, Switzerland, where I completed both my bachelor's in physics and my master's in theoretical physics. Alongside the core physics curriculum, I developed a growing appreciation for the structure and rigour of mathematics, which led me to take a number of additional courses in that field.

Among the most formative experiences were the lectures on classical field theory and general relativity by Matthias Blau. These sparked my enduring interest in the geometry of spacetime and the mathematical structure of physical theories. I later wrote my master's thesis under his supervision, which gave me the opportunity to engage with relativity and gravitation more deeply and hands-on—an experience that cemented my conviction that this is the right field for me.

Parallel to my theoretical interests, I have cultivated a strong interest in high-performance computing, particularly using graphics processors and CUDA. My bachelor's thesis and various subsequent programming projects helped me build substantial proficiency in C++ and CUDA and gradually nudged me towards the direction of numerical relativity. This fusion of mathematical physics and computational methods is at the core of my current research interests.

Following this foundation, I completed the Part III MASt in theoretical physics at the University of Cambridge (2025–2026). This intense year allowed me to further deepen my understanding of gravitation, differential geometry, and advanced mathematical physics. My performance across the curriculum culminated in graduating with Distinction (ranking 8th out of 284 students overall) and being awarded the Mayhew Prize for the greatest distinction in Applied Mathematics and Theoretical Physics.

This winter, I will take the next step in my research career by beginning a PhD in numerical relativity at the University of Nottingham under the supervision of Miguel Bezares and Thomas Sotiriou. This doctoral position will allow me to merge my background in general relativity with my experience in high-performance computing, and I aim to conduct research in the development of parabolic initial data solvers and evolution methods for higher-order theories of metrised gravity.

## Research Interests and Career Vision

My research interests center on the mathematical structure, initial data formulation, and numerical evolution of modified and higher-order theories of gravity. I am driven by the entire pipeline of the problem: from rigorous geometric foundations to the design and implementation of parallelized numerical relativity codes capable of simulating strong-field, compact-source dynamics where extensions to General Relativity manifest.

On the initial data front, I am particularly interested in developing versatile parabolic and diffusion-type equation solvers. Because these methods allow a natural, robust implementation of gradient descent optimization directly mapping to the structural functionals derived from the theory's constraints, they offer unparalleled flexibility when adapting to varying gravitational actions. My goal is to leverage these diffusion methodologies to construct robust initial data frameworks that bypass the rigidity of traditional elliptic reduction schemes when moving beyond standard spacetimes.

On the evolution side, my focus is directed toward higher-order curvature invariants, specifically Quadratic Gravity. Long recognized for its power-counting renormalizability, the theory has seen a major modern resurgence following recent proofs by Turok and Bateman demonstrating that its historic classical instabilities and ghost states can be consistently resolved and unitarized at the quantum level. Simulating the non-linear classical regimes of such higher-derivative theories requires an evolution infrastructure designed specifically for complex, auxiliary-variable hyperbolic reductions.

Outside of academia, I enjoy engaging with more hands-on, craft-oriented projects. Electronics has fascinated me since childhood—what began with assembling simple amplifier kits gradually evolved into more complex builds, such as designing and constructing my own mechanical keyboard, complete with a CNC-machined aluminium chassis.
